The 5 Best Trading Psychology Books for Futures Traders (And What Each One Actually Fixes)

Most trading psychology books tell you to control your emotions. That advice is why you keep blowing up.

This post covers the five books that actually changed how I execute, what specific problem each one solves, and the order I'd read them in. You'll leave knowing which one to buy first based on the mistake you keep repeating.

I'm Jason. I'm a futures trader and a trading coach. I'm also a professional orchestral percussionist, which means I've spent a career walking into rooms where one bad decision under pressure ends the day. Six years ago I understood nothing about the mental side of this. I've read north of thirty books on trading and performance since. Most were fine. These five weren't.

What are the best trading psychology books for futures traders?

The best trading psychology books for futures traders are Tendler's The Mental Game of Trading, Murphy's Inner Excellence, Hougaard's The Best Loser Wins, and Chakrapani's How to Be a Stoic When You Don't Know How. Pair them with one technical book: Sklarew's Techniques of a Professional Commodity Chart Analyst.

Four books for your head. One for your charts. Here's what each one actually does.

1. The Mental Game of Trading, Jared Tendler

This is the book that broke the “control your emotions” myth for me.

Tendler's core argument: you don't control emotions, you resolve them. Fear and tilt aren't malfunctions to suppress. They're symptoms pointing at a flaw in your thinking that you haven't found yet. Suppress the symptom and it comes back bigger, usually at 9:45 in the morning with three contracts on.

Two ideas from this book changed how I work.

The first is the mental hand history. You map, in writing, what you were thinking, what you were feeling, and what you actually did, then work backward to the flawed belief underneath. It is the emotional version of reviewing a losing trade. For process-driven people it's a gift, because it turns “I got emotional” into a specific, correctable line item.

The second is the inchworm concept, and I think every futures trader needs it. Everyone obsesses over their A game. They want a higher ceiling. Tendler's point is that your range matters more than your peak, and the fastest gains come from making your C game less catastrophic. One of my mentors put it this way: practice so your worst day is better than most people's best day. Raise the floor and your account stops taking body blows.

No hype, no affirmations. If discipline and consistency are your bottleneck, start here.

2. Inner Excellence, Jim Murphy

This one went to the top of the bestseller lists after Eagles receiver A.J. Brown was photographed reading it on the sideline mid-game, which is a very funny way for a performance psychology book to break out.

Read it in tandem with Tendler. Here's the split:

  • The Mental Game of Trading helps you diagnose and correct what breaks down during execution.
  • Inner Excellence helps you build the ability to be fully present by letting go of the outcome.

Murphy's argument is that elite performance comes from performing for the sake of the performance, not for the result. For an athlete, the championship is a byproduct of total presence in the rep. For us, the equity curve is a byproduct of executing the setup in front of you without simultaneously running a spreadsheet in your head about what this trade does to your month.

If you've ever widened a stop because you needed the day to be green, this is your book.

3. Techniques of a Professional Commodity Chart Analyst, Arthur Sklarew

Complete opposite of everything above, and it lives on my desk.

It's dry. It's technical. It is pure structure and pattern recognition, and that's the point. What it gave me was a working understanding of classical chart patterns, triangles, head and shoulders, double tops, and the measured price objectives that go with each one.

That matters because a price objective turns a chart pattern into a plan. You're not guessing where to take profit. You have a defined level, a defined invalidation, and a defined risk before you click anything.

This book earned its own section in the checklist I use to plan every trade idea.

4. The Best Loser Wins, Tom Hougaard

Hougaard pairs with Murphy, and the contrast is the most useful thing on this list:

  • Inner Excellence teaches presence by letting go of the future.
  • The Best Loser Wins teaches presence by letting go of the past.

Losses are not a bug in futures trading. They're the cost of doing business. The damage almost never comes from the loss itself. It comes from the next three trades, the ones you take at double size because you want the money back before the close.

Hougaard is blunt about this in a way most authors aren't, partly because he trades size and talks openly about what it does to him. If a red trade contaminates your next hour, this is the book.

5. How to Be a Stoic When You Don't Know How, Chuck Chakrapani

My gateway into Stoicism, and a good place for anyone to start.

It's short, practical, and structured around weekly techniques and practice goals rather than philosophy lectures. The whole thing trains one skill: focusing only on what you control.

In futures, that list is shorter than you think. You control your size, your entry criteria, your stop, whether you take the trade at all, and whether you take another one after a loss. You control none of the rest. Frustration, impatience, and overreaction all come from arguing with the part you don't control.

If you only have one weekend, read this one. It's the lowest cost of entry and it changes how you sit through a chop day.

What order should you read these trading psychology books in?

Read in the order your problems show up, not the order I listed them.

  • You keep repeating the same emotional mistake: start with The Mental Game of Trading.
  • You can't stop trading after a loss: start with The Best Loser Wins.
  • You manage the P&L instead of the trade: start with Inner Excellence.
  • You want the shortest possible on-ramp: start with How to Be a Stoic When You Don't Know How.
  • Your plan is vague and your exits are vibes: start with Sklarew.

One warning. Reading is not practice. A book you underline and never apply is entertainment. Pick one concept per book, write it into your session prep, and review it against your actual fills at the end of the week.

FAQ

What is the single best book on trading psychology?

For most futures traders, The Mental Game of Trading by Jared Tendler. It's the most systematic and the least motivational, and it gives you a repeatable process for finding the belief underneath a recurring mistake. Start there unless revenge trading is your specific problem.

Do trading psychology books actually help you become profitable?

They help you stop sabotaging a plan you already have. They cannot make a bad plan good, and no book will tell you what to trade. Treat them as tools for execution and self-analysis, not as strategies.

Is The Best Loser Wins worth reading?

Yes, if losses affect your next trade. Hougaard's focus is on emotional recovery after a red trade rather than on setups or entries. It's a short read and it pairs well with Inner Excellence.

How many trading books should I read?

Fewer than you think. I've read more than thirty and I actively use five. Rereading one book you apply beats skimming ten you don't.

Do I need a technical analysis book if I already have a strategy?

Only if your exits are vague. Sklarew's value is in measured price objectives, which give you a defined target and a defined invalidation before entry. If you can already state both in numbers, you can skip it.
